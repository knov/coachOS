Progress tracking transforms your session notes from a log into a narrative of change. By explicitly tracking goals, milestones, and shifts, you help clients see how far they've come — which is often invisible to them in the day-to-day. The accumulation of small moves only becomes visible when someone names it, and that's one of the most powerful things you can do as a coach.

## What to Track

Progress tracking covers multiple dimensions of change — because growth rarely shows up in just one place at once.

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Goals">
    The stated goals from intake, and how they evolve over the engagement. Goals often sharpen, deepen, or shift entirely as a client does the work. Tracking these changes tells its own story.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Milestones">
    Specific achievements or moments of meaningful forward movement — a difficult conversation a client finally had, a decision they made, a pattern they broke. Milestones don't have to be large to be worth recording.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Recurring Patterns">
    Themes, beliefs, or behaviors that keep showing up across sessions. Naming a pattern is the first step toward shifting it. Your record of recurring patterns is one of the most useful things you bring to a client mid-engagement.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Shifts and Breakthroughs">
    Significant mindset or behavior changes you observe — the moments when something genuinely moves. These are distinct from milestones: they're often internal, subtle, and not something the client might think to report as progress.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Commitments Completion Rate">
    How consistently the client follows through on between-session actions. This isn't about judgment — it's data. Low follow-through often points to something worth exploring in the coaching itself: misalignment, fear, overcommitment, or unclear motivation.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

## Setting Up Progress Tracking

Start progress tracking from day one. A baseline captured at intake makes the eventual closing summary dramatically more powerful.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Capture Baseline Goals">
    At intake, record the client's stated goals and their own sense of where they currently are. Ask them to describe the gap — where they are versus where they want to be — in their own words.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Agree on Milestones">
    With the client, identify what meaningful progress looks like at the midpoint and end of the engagement. These become shared reference points that you can return to together.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Log Progress in Session Notes">
    Use the Insights and Shifts section of your session notes to capture real-time progress. You don't need a separate system — consistent session notes are your progress record.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Conduct a Midpoint Review">
    Halfway through the engagement, explicitly review goals together. Note what's shifted, what's still live, and what's changed in how the client sees the original challenge. Re-contract if needed.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Create a Closing Summary">
    At the end of the engagement, synthesize the full arc of progress into a closing summary. This becomes a record of the work — and a powerful gift to the client as they move forward.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Sharing Progress with the Client

One of the most impactful things you can do as a coach is reflect progress back to clients at the right moment. Many clients don't register how much they've changed until it's named explicitly. The person who walked in six months ago had a different relationship with conflict, with ambition, with their own voice — and they often can't see that from the inside.

Your progress notes give you the material to do this well. When you can say, "In our first session you described yourself as someone who always backs down — and last week you told your board no," you're not motivating or cheerleading. You're witnessing. That's different, and it lands differently.

<Note>
  Your progress notes are internal coaching records, not documents shared verbatim with clients. Use them to inform what you surface and celebrate in the coaching conversation — not as a report to hand over.
</Note>

## Using Progress Data to Improve Your Practice

Your client progress records are also a development tool for you. Patterns across multiple clients' progress data reveal where your coaching is most powerful — the kinds of shifts you reliably help people make — and where clients consistently get stuck or plateau.

Pay attention to where progress stalls across different clients. Is it at the same stage of the engagement? On similar themes? When clients are asked to take a specific kind of action? These patterns are an invitation to evolve your frameworks, deepen your skills, and bring more of what's working into the work you do.
