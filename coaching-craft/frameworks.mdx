Frameworks are the structured approaches you bring to coaching conversations — the models, methodologies, and mental maps that give shape to your work. Most experienced coaches carry a rich internal library of these, developed through training, reading, and hard-won practice. The problem is that tacit knowledge stays locked inside you. Documenting your frameworks turns that implicit expertise into an explicit library you can refine over time, apply more deliberately, and draw on consistently across every client engagement.

## Types of Frameworks to Document

Your framework library will span several categories. Documenting across all of them gives you a complete picture of the structured knowledge you bring to your coaching practice.

<CardGroup cols={2}>
  <Card title="Conversation Models" icon="comments">
    Frameworks for structuring a coaching conversation from opening to close. These include well-known models like GROW, CLEAR, and OSKAR, as well as any proprietary or hybrid structures you've developed yourself. Conversation models help you move through a session with direction and coherence.
  </Card>

  <Card title="Assessment Tools" icon="chart-bar">
    Models for diagnosing where a client is — in their life, their work, their development. Examples include the Wheel of Life, energy audits, values inventories, and readiness assessments. These tools help you and the client establish a shared picture of current reality before moving into action.
  </Card>

  <Card title="Intervention Frameworks" icon="bolt">
    Specific techniques and approaches for common coaching situations — working with a stuck client, navigating resistance, facilitating a breakthrough, or helping a client reframe a limiting belief. These are the moves you reach for when a session needs a deliberate shift in direction or energy.
  </Card>

  <Card title="Domain Frameworks" icon="layer-group">
    Subject-matter models relevant to your coaching niche. If you coach leaders, this might include models of organizational change, psychological safety, or influence. If you coach career transitions, it might include career anchors or job crafting frameworks. These give your coaching intellectual depth and credibility within your domain.
  </Card>
</CardGroup>

## How to Document a Framework

Consistency in how you document frameworks makes your library far more usable. Rather than scattered notes, a standard template ensures every entry gives you what you need when you're preparing for a session or deciding which approach to use.

For each framework you document, capture the following:

- **Name and source** — what it's called and where it comes from (book, training, your own development)
- **Purpose** — what this framework is designed to accomplish and the type of situation it addresses
- **Key phases or steps** — the core structure, broken down into its distinct components
- **Example questions or prompts per phase** — the specific language you use to move through each stage
- **When it works well / when it doesn't** — the conditions under which this framework is most effective, and where it tends to fall short
- **Your personal variations or adaptations** — how you've modified or extended the original model based on your experience

<Info>
  Even frameworks you didn't invent benefit enormously from your notes on how you adapt and apply them. A documented GROW model that includes your preferred question variations and the client situations where you've found it most powerful is far more valuable than a clean description of the original model.
</Info>

## Evolving Your Frameworks

Your framework library should be treated as a living set of documents, not a static reference shelf. Each time you use a framework in a session, you have an opportunity to learn something about it — what worked, what fell flat, what question landed differently than expected, what adaptation you made on the fly that you want to remember.

Build a habit of returning to a framework's entry after sessions where you deployed it. Add a brief note: what you tried, how the client responded, what you'd do differently. Over time, these accumulated observations transform a borrowed model into something genuinely yours — a version shaped by your clients, your instincts, and your experience.

When you discover something significant about how a framework performs — or when you develop a new variation worth preserving — log that insight in your growth practice first, then bring it back to update the framework entry. See [Capturing Insights](/growth/capturing-insights) for how to build that habit into your regular practice.

## Your Framework Library

The Frameworks section of Coaching OS is the home for all of your documented models, organized in a way that makes sense for how you work. You might organize by type (conversation models, assessments, interventions), by client situation (clarity, transitions, leadership challenges), or by how frequently you use them. The right structure is the one that makes it fast and intuitive to find what you need.

Think of this library as one of the highest-leverage assets in your practice. A well-maintained framework library makes your coaching more consistent, accelerates your development as a practitioner, and gives you a concrete artifact of the expertise you've built over your career.
