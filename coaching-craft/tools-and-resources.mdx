Every coach builds a toolkit over time — assessments, exercises, readings, visual models, and worksheets that support specific coaching situations. Some tools come from formal training. Others emerge from a book you read, a peer coaching conversation, or a moment of spontaneous experimentation that worked. This section of Coaching OS is your personal library: the place where everything you draw on is catalogued, described, and ready to use when the moment calls for it.

## Categories of Coaching Tools

A well-rounded toolkit spans several categories. Knowing what you have across all of them helps you match the right resource to the right client need, rather than defaulting to familiar favorites out of habit.

<CardGroup cols={2}>
  <Card title="Assessments" icon="clipboard-list">
    Personality tools, strengths inventories, values assessments, and 360 feedback instruments. Assessments give clients structured language for self-understanding and create a shared map of who they are and how they operate. Used well, they accelerate the early stages of coaching and surface blind spots the client hasn't named for themselves.
  </Card>

  <Card title="Exercises & Activities" icon="pen-to-square">
    Journaling prompts, visualization scripts, somatic practices, future-self letters, values card sorts, and other experiential activities. These tools engage the client beyond conversation — bringing in the body, the imagination, and different ways of knowing. They're especially useful when a client is stuck in their head and needs a different mode of access.
  </Card>

  <Card title="Worksheets & Templates" icon="table-columns">
    Structured reflection tools, goal-setting templates, decision matrices, planning canvases, and other formats the client can work with between sessions. Well-designed worksheets extend the coaching conversation into the client's daily life and help them build habits of reflection independent of your time together.
  </Card>

  <Card title="Reference Materials" icon="books">
    Books, articles, models, frameworks, and other resources you reference regularly or assign to clients. These form the intellectual scaffolding of your coaching practice — the sources you return to for grounding, inspiration, or client recommendations. Keeping them catalogued saves you from recommending resources you can only half-remember.
  </Card>
</CardGroup>

## How to Catalogue a Tool

A consistent cataloguing format makes your library genuinely useful rather than just comprehensive. For each tool you add, capture the following:

- **Name** — what you call it (including any original title if it has one)
- **Source / author** — where it comes from and who created it
- **Purpose** — what this tool is designed to accomplish and what client need it addresses
- **When to use it** — the situations, stages, or client states where this tool is most likely to be valuable
- **How you introduce it** — the framing you use to present it to a client so it lands well
- **Notes from using it** — what you've learned about this tool in practice: what works, what to watch for, how different clients have responded

<Note>
  Note which clients you've used each tool with — in their individual client profile, not here. That cross-reference ensures you don't accidentally repeat an assessment a client has already completed, and it lets you track patterns over time: which tools seem to resonate most with which kinds of clients, and what combinations produce the best results.
</Note>

## Matching Tools to Client Needs

The ability to select the right tool for the right moment is one of the subtler craft skills in coaching. Not every session needs a tool. In fact, reaching for a tool too quickly can interrupt a client's natural process — adding structure when what the situation calls for is space.

Developing your intuition for tool deployment means asking: What does this client need right now that conversation alone isn't providing? Is this a moment for a different kind of engagement — more structured, more experiential, more reflective? Would a visual model help us think together, or would it take us away from what's alive in the room?

As your library grows, maintain a short "favorites" list — the five to ten tools you reach for most reliably across many clients and situations. These are your workhorses: proven, versatile, and well-practiced enough that you can deploy them with ease. Having this list available during pre-session preparation means you can quickly consider whether any of them are relevant without scanning your entire library.

## Building Your Library Over Time

Your toolkit is never finished — it grows with you. Every training, workshop, peer coaching conversation, or supervision session is a potential source of new tools, new framings of familiar tools, or new insights about tools you already use.

<Tip>
  After any training or workshop, add tool discoveries to your library immediately while the context and your enthusiasm are fresh. Even a partial entry — just a name, source, and brief note on purpose — is enough to plant a flag you can return to and complete later. Tools you mean to document "someday" rarely get documented at all.
</Tip>

When you have a significant discovery about a tool — a new way to introduce it, an unexpected use case, a client response that reframed how you think about it — log it in your growth practice first, then bring it back to update the tool's library entry. See [Capturing Insights](/growth/capturing-insights) for how to build that reflection habit into your regular rhythm, so your toolkit keeps getting sharper over time.
